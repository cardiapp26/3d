## 1. Septal anatomy relevant to puncture

- **Fossa ovalis.** It is oval or round, about **1.5–2.4 cm²** in area, and made mostly of thin fibrous tissue. It is the easiest and safest place for a standard puncture. The rest of the septum is thicker and mostly atrial muscle, but it can still be crossed. [TS, "Anteroposterior", p.16]
- **Limbus (limbic edge).** This is the ridge where septum secundum overlaps septum primum and usually fuses with it. When the tip drops over this ridge during the pull-back from the SVC, it has entered the fossa, which is the safe crossing zone. Fig. 9 shows the superior limbic edge on a pathology specimen. [TS, p.13, Fig. 9]
- **Unfused primum and secundum (PFO-like).** The needle or catheter may pass with little or no upward pressure. [TS, p.14]
- **Effect of LA size.** In an enlarged LA the fossa tends to move **inferiorly** (Fig. 12 shows small, medium and large LA). In valve disease the fossa may shift, and a puncture outside it can still succeed. [TS, p.16, Fig. 12]
- **Aorta and non-coronary cusp (NCC), anterior to the fossa.** In the lateral view a pigtail sits deep in the aortic sinus to mark the aortic root. The needle should point medially or slightly posteriorly, which keeps it away from the aorta anteriorly and the RA posterior wall posteriorly. In the EP lab, a quadripolar catheter recording a His electrogram marks the **inferior aspect of the NCC**. [TS, "Lateral", p.17; "Technical considerations during EP procedures", p.32]
- **Coronary sinus (CS).** A multipolar CS catheter outlines the AV groove and marks the **caudal (lower) end** of the septum. Together with the His catheter it shows how the heart is rotated. [TS, "LAO", p.17; p.32]
- **Tricuspid valve.** In Hung's method, a pigtail placed deep in the right coronary sinus lies close to the **upper end of the tricuspid valve**, which serves as a fluoroscopic reference. [TS, p.15, Fig. 11B]
- **Posterior side.** If the puncture is too far back and outside the fossa, the needle can exit into the **pericardial space between the two atria**. This is a particular risk in a giant LA. [TS, "Giant left atrium", p.27]
- **LAA.** Its orifice usually lies almost straight across the LA from the fossa. The appendage points superiorly and usually anteriorly. [TS, "Closure of the LAA", p.37]
- **Gap.** The chapter does not use the terms "true vs false septum" and gives no measured distances to the tricuspid annulus or the CS ostium. For those, add an anatomy source (for example the Simard 2021 JACC CI paper, which the chapter cites as ref 34).

## 2. Step-by-step technique (fluoroscopy-led)

- **Equipment.** Brockenbrough needle: the original Ross needle narrowed from **18 G to 21 G over its distal 1.5 cm** for safety. An arrow on the hub shows which way the curve points (Fig. 1). The needle goes inside a Mullins or Swartz sheath (Fig. 2). Other needles: BRK series with various curves, "ES" extra-sharp versions, the Endrys coaxial double needle, and the SafeSept **0.014"** wire, which forms a J in the LA so it does not hit the posterior wall. [TS, "Equipment", p.3–6]
- **Patient.** Supine with an IV line. General anaesthesia is usually needed when TOE guides the puncture. [TS, p.12]
- **Pressure line.** Many operators attach the needle hub to a pressure line so the change from RA to LA trace shows at once. Many also keep a pigtail in the right coronary sinus to mark the aorta. Ask the patient not to cough at the moment of puncture. [TS, p.14]
- **SVC descent.** Pass the sheath over a wire into the SVC, then advance the needle until it sits just inside the dilator tip. Point the curve to about **4 o'clock** as seen from below (up to **6 o'clock** for a very large LA). Pull the assembly down slowly; it moves medially as it enters the RA. [TS, p.13]
- **Two "jumps".** Do the descent in the AP view and watch for two jumps: SVC to RA, then RA over the limbus into the fossa. The second jump is a small shift that you see and/or feel. It is found in **over 90%** of structurally normal hearts and is the standard landmark in EP cases. [TS, p.13–14, p.17]
- **Catch point.** Pull back slightly, push upward to engage the septum, and optionally check in the lateral view that the needle points posteriorly. Hold the sheath still and advance the needle; a small "give" means it has crossed. [TS, p.13]
- **Confirm LA entry.** Keep the assembly still until at least **two of three** signs are present: an LA pressure trace, aspiration of bright red oxygenated blood, or contrast swirling in the LA. Then advance the sheath and immediately rotate it to **3 o'clock** to keep clear of the posterior LA wall. [TS, p.13]
- **Contrast map when the limbus is not felt (dilated LA, structural disease).** Inject about **30 mL at 12 mL/s** into the RA and follow the contrast into the LA. This shows where the two atria overlap; note that area against the vertebrae. [TS, p.14, Fig. 10]
- **Inoue method.** Visualise the LA with contrast. Draw an imaginary horizontal line from the top of the tricuspid valve to the right edge of the LA, then drop a vertical line from its midpoint. Puncture on that vertical line, a little less than one vertebral-body height above the lower LA edge. **Hung method:** the same lines on plain fluoroscopy, starting from a pigtail in the right coronary sinus. [TS, p.15, Fig. 11]
- **RAO 40–50°.** The atria are superimposed and seen face-on. The fossa is usually **1–3 cm below** the pigtail tip in the aortic root and lies between the pigtail and the back edge of the RA shadow. [TS, "RAO", p.17]
- **LAO.** You look along the septum edge-on. The CS catheter marks the lower end and the pigtail or His catheter marks the upper end. Adjust LAO until the His catheter is end-on; the needle should then be at right angles to the septum (Fig. 23). [TS, p.17, p.32]
- **Lateral.** Mark the aortic root with a pigtail. Imagine a horizontal line from the aortic root to the spine; the puncture site is **3–5 mm below** that line (Fig. 13). At the level of the aortic root, switch briefly to RAO to confirm the needle points posteriorly. [TS, p.17–18]
- **Heparin timing.** Give a bolus after the puncture: usually **3000–5000 U**, or up to **100 U/kg**. In AF ablation, the chapter states that puncture now goes ahead on uninterrupted VKA with heparin added, and that NOACs are not held. In MitraClip under TOE, experienced operators can puncture with **INR >2**. In general, the chapter advises avoiding the procedure in bleeding diathesis or **INR >1.5**. If IV heparin is running, stop it **4 h** before and restart it **2 h** after. [TS, "Contraindications", p.10]
- **Long attempts.** If the puncture drags on, pull the needle out every **4–6 min** and back-flush the sheath to clear any thrombus forming on the steel needle. [TS, p.30]

## 3. Imaging guidance (TOE / ICE)

- **When echo is needed.** Most routine punctures in experienced hands do not need echo. It is essential when a specific crossing site is required (LAAO, edge-to-edge repair, long-tunnel PFO, mitral PVL, PV stenting), when anatomy is unusual, and for less experienced operators. The reported success rate is **99%** with few complications. [TS, "Echographic guidance", p.18–20, Fig. 14]
- **Shared terms (Fig. 15).**
  - Superior = towards the SVC; inferior = towards the IVC.
  - Anterior = towards the aorta; posterior = away from the aorta.
  - Low = near the mitral valve; high = far from it. [TS, p.19]
- **Three TOE planes (Fig. 16).**
  - Bicaval at **90–120°** sets superior vs inferior.
  - Basal short axis at **30–50°** sets anterior vs posterior.
  - Four-chamber at **0°** measures height above the mitral valve, which is mandatory for MitraClip and other mitral procedures. [TS, p.21]
- **Tenting.** The needle tip shows as a tent-like bulge of the septum into the LA (Fig. 17). An **abrupt loss of tenting** confirms the crossing. 3D X-plane shows short and long axes together. [TS, p.21–22]
- **ICE (Fig. 18).** Find tenting in the long-axis view and get a clear image of the point of maximal tent before puncturing. Rotate the ICE catheter counter-clockwise to see the aortic root. Confirm the sheath is in the LA with saline or contrast, or by a left-to-right colour shunt. ICE is monoplane and costly but needs no general anaesthesia. About **50%** of the experts on the 2017/2018 AF-ablation consensus panel use it routinely. A micro-TOE probe (**7.5 mm** tip) allows conscious sedation, but images are poorer. [TS, p.20–22]
- **Puncture site by procedure (Fig. 14).**
  - **MitraClip / edge-to-edge:** superior-posterior fossa.
    - Primary MR: **4–5 cm** above the mitral annulus.
    - Secondary MR: lower, about **3.5 cm** above the annular plane, or **4–4.5 cm** above leaflet coaptation.
    - Ideal four-chamber distance to the coaptation line: **3.5–4 cm**. [TS, p.36]
  - **Wrong MitraClip site:**
    - too anterior (for example through a PFO): "aortic hugging";
    - too high: cannot cross the mitral valve;
    - too low: cannot pull back and grasp the leaflets;
    - too posterior: perforation risk. [TS, p.36–37]
  - **Valve-in-valve, valve-in-ring, annuloplasty:** like MitraClip, superior and posterior. [TS, p.37]
  - **LAAO:** most often **inferior and posterior**, for coaxial entry into the appendage. Turn the sheath slightly counter-clockwise for an anterior orifice. [TS, p.37]
  - **Balloon mitral commissurotomy:** centre of the fossa or, preferably, slightly **low**. If the valve cannot be crossed, redo the puncture higher and more posterior. [TS, p.38]
  - **Mitral PVL:** some operators go high for lateral leaks (6–12 o'clock) and lower for septal leaks (12–6 o'clock); others always go high. [TS, p.37]
  - **Long-tunnel PFO:** puncture the most cephalad part of septum primum at the fossa, so the device clamps primum and secundum together. [TS, "PFO closure", p.35]
  - **AF ablation:** even a **1 cm** change in site can change how easily two LA catheters can be steered. [TS, p.20]
- **CT planning.** For LAAO and PVL closure, MSCT with 3D reconstruction (for example 3mensio) can plan the most direct puncture site (Fig. 8). [TS, "Imaging before the procedure", p.11]
- **Before the procedure.** TTE: the apical four-chamber view shows septal thickness and curvature, any aneurysm or myxoma, and any baseline pericardial effusion, to compare against if bleeding is suspected later. TOE is recommended for everyone to exclude LA/LAA thrombus. [TS, p.11]

## 4. Difficult septum and tools

- **List of difficult septa (Focus box 3).** Resistant, tender, bulging, giant LA, very large RA, atrial septal aneurysm. [TS, p.25]
- **Resistant or thick septum.** More likely after previous punctures, cardiac surgery or chest radiation, and in older patients. The needle may be on muscular septum rather than the fossa, so consider choosing a new site.
  - The danger is stored forward force. When the septum finally gives, needle, dilator and sheath can lunge across the LA and perforate it.
  - To avoid this, advance the needle alone until the pressure trace turns to LA. [TS, "Resistant septum", p.25–26]
- **Radiofrequency (RF) options.**
  - **NRG RF needle** (Baylis): blunt tip, **5 W for 2–5 s**, usually crosses after **1–4 pulses**. Needs little pushing and works on thick, scarred or calcified septa and possibly on surgical patches (Fig. 5).
  - **VersaCross:** an RF wire that turns into a pigtail once in the LA, and a reshapeable catheter that can be pushed back up to the SVC safely (Fig. 6).
  - **Electrocautery:** brief pulses from a standard surgical generator, clipped to the Brockenbrough hub.
  - Use any of these only when TOE or ICE clearly shows tenting at the target. [TS, p.5–6, p.26]
- **Needle crosses but the sheath does not.**
  - Pass a **0.014"** exchange angioplasty wire through the needle into the **left superior PV** as a rail.
  - Or loop the wire inside the LA, so the sheath tracks over the loop instead of shooting across.
  - If the device itself will not cross (Inoue balloon, MitraClip), dilate the hole with an **8–14 mm** balloon sized to the device. [TS, p.26]
- **Contrast stain check when stuck.** Inject a tiny puff through the needle: a vertical stain suggests the tip is too high; a horizontal stain suggests it is too low. [TS, p.26]
- **Bulging septum** (high LA pressure, for example severe MS). The tip slides off the convexity; torque it firmly back onto the middle of the bulge. [TS, p.27]
- **Giant LA.** It is often harder, not easier. Go unusually low but not too posterior, because a puncture behind the fossa can exit into the space between the atria. [TS, p.27]
- **Very large RA.** Bend the needle about **10 cm from its tip**, or use a needle with a larger curve (BRK1 instead of BRK) (Fig. 19). [TS, p.27]
- **Atrial septal aneurysm.** Often easy to cross, since a PFO is more common. If it resists, the aneurysm can bulge into the LA under pressure and bring the needle close to the posterior wall. Protect the wall with the SafeSept J-wire or an angioplasty wire passed through the needle (Fig. 20). [TS, p.28]
- **Re-starting the pull-down.** Use the **anterior staircase**: from the low position, turn the curve to **12 o'clock**, walk the tip up while sweeping side to side, then turn back posterior and pull down again (Fig. 21). [TS, p.29]
- **Previous patch or closure device.**
  - Puncture through an intra-atrial patch is reported as safe.
  - Successful puncture through an Amplatzer septal occluder has been reported.
  - The only absolute contraindications are thrombus on the septum and myxoma or other tumour of the septum. [TS, "Contraindications", p.7–8]
- **Other anatomy.**
  - Azygos continuation of the IVC means femoral access is impossible; use the SVC or a transhepatic route. The tip sits abnormally far back (Fig. 7).
  - Dextrocardia with situs inversus: left femoral access, image inversion and TOE.
  - Lumbar spine arthritis can stop the needle at the iliac crest. Pull the sheath back, advance the needle to the obstruction, then let the sheath carry it past. [TS, p.8–10, p.30]
- **Alternative routes.**
  - Left femoral vein (may help reach a higher site for edge-to-edge repair).
  - Right internal jugular with a paediatric Brockenbrough or Endrys needle.
  - Transhepatic: 22 G Chiba needle, 0.018" wire, 9–10 Fr sheath, 8 Fr Mullins; the tract is closed with coils or a vascular plug. [TS, p.23–24]

## 5. Complications: recognition and management

- **List (Focus box 6).** Atrial arrhythmias, vasovagal reaction, embolism, puncture outside the atria, haemopericardium, post-puncture ASD. [TS, p.38]
- **Puncture outside the atria (including aortic or free-wall puncture).**
  - Sign: no LA pressure after advancing. The risk is higher with a resistant septum or a small LA.
  - **Needle-only perforation** can be withdrawn safely. **Sheath perforation** almost always means major bleeding and needs surgical removal.
  - How to tell them apart:
    1. Inject **0.1–0.3 mL** of contrast through the needle. A stain outside the atrium confirms needle perforation.
    2. Pull the needle back to just inside the sheath tip and inject again.
    3. If contrast swirls in the atrium, the sheath is inside and can be withdrawn. Further staining means surgery.
  - Reported bail-outs: downsizing over a wire into the aorta under echo, or **percutaneous Amplatzer closure of an aortic puncture**. [TS, p.39–40]
- **Haemopericardium / tamponade.**
  - First signs: falling blood pressure and reduced motion of the heart border on fluoroscopy.
  - Small or moderate and stable: watch closely. Tamponade threatening: echo-guided pericardiocentesis at once; the blood can be autotransfused. Not stabilising: emergency surgery.
  - Historical series: **21/1765 (1.2%)** tamponade; 18 settled with medical measures, 2 needed surgery and 2 died.
  - TTE must be immediately available. [TS, p.40]
- **Crossing outside the heart through the inter-atrial cleft** (enlarged atria). It shows as severe bradycardia and hypotension from mediastinal bleeding after a crossing that looked successful. **Do not pull the sheath back**; send the patient to surgery, because removal can cause exsanguination on the table. [TS, "Vasovagal reaction", p.39]
- **Posterior LA wall.** Risks: a too-posterior puncture, the lunge across a resistant septum, and a septal aneurysm bulging into the LA. Protective steps: turn the sheath to 3 o'clock after crossing, advance the needle alone first, use RF, and use a J-wire or angioplasty wire. [TS, p.13, p.26–28]
- **Air and thrombo-embolism.**
  - Take great care to keep air out of the needle and sheath, and back-flush regularly in long procedures.
  - ICE found thrombi in **10.3%** of AF ablations, mostly on sheaths or catheters.
  - Transient inferior ST elevation (Bezold-Jarisch-like reflex) with normal coronaries has been reported. The chapter says micro-air embolism cannot be excluded as the cause.
  - The chapter does not describe a specific air-embolism treatment protocol. [TS, p.39]
- **Vasovagal / Bezold-Jarisch-like reflex.** Severe bradycardia and hypotension that can resist treatment, thought to come from irritating nerve fibres in the septum. [TS, p.39]
- **Atrial arrhythmias.** Triggered by the wire or catheter; usually stop on their own, and IV antiarrhythmics are occasionally needed. [TS, p.39]
- **Iatrogenic ASD.**
  - TOE or ICE shows a small left-to-right shunt after almost every puncture.
  - Only **15%** persist at 6 months, and persistence tracks sheath size.
  - Closure is uncommon. Consider it for a large defect, right-to-left shunting with hypoxia, or volume overload. [TS, p.40]
- **Children.** A thick, fibrous septum can make the needle jump from RA to LA, injure the atrial wall and cause tamponade. [TS, p.30]
